# Appendix C: Pronunciation and intonation

## Pronunciation
Communication depends upon mutual intelligibility. Ease of communication depends largely on the extent to which speaker and listener share a common practice. For purposes of national or international communication, standard languages with standard pronunciations have generally developed.

In Britain, this role is played by Received Pronunciation (RP) as codified by D. Jones, A. C. Gimson, and others, and generally adopted by broadcasting authorities, dictionary makers, and course designers. On a global scale, English is polycentric; Ireland, the USA, Canada, and Australia have their own standard norms, which are fully mutually intelligible. In a European learning context, it remains standard to adhere to the European variety (British norm).

By Vantage level, phonetic development from Threshold is primarily one of increased facility and unconscious mastery, as articulatory and auditory skills become thoroughly habituated so that low-level processes are automatized. This allows for:
* a closer approximation to the sound of native speech at a sub-phonemic level;
* greater ease in concatenating longer sound sequences;
* an easier, natural use of the **phonetic reduction** normal in native colloquial usage, including:
  * *vowel reduction* in unstressed syllables;
  * *consonantal elision* (e.g., elision of [t] or [d] in context: *first thing*, *Round Church*, *handbag*, *handgun*);
  * *consonantal assimilation* (e.g., replacement of t/d/n by p/b/m before bilabials, or by k, g, ng before velars: *goodbye*, *good girl*).

Vantage learners are aware of and avoid inappropriate reductions. As listeners, they can recognize standard variants (RP and General American) spoken at normal conversational speed (up to about 150 words per minute) and can follow highly reduced familiar registers or slight accents using compensation strategies.

## Intonation
The intonation of English is used by native speakers to indicate the informational structure of sentences, express nuances of meaning, indicate unspoken implications or reservations, and convey emotional states. Within the rhythmic tone group, pitch prominence focuses on two critical locations: the head and the nucleus. The last prominent stressed syllable in a tone group is its **nucleus**, which initiates a pitch glide pattern that continues to the end of the group. Five nuclear tones are distinguished at Vantage level:

### 1 Low falling [ \` ]
Starts on a clear, low-mid tone and drops to a low creaky note. Used for:
* **Declarative sentences**: factual statements, identifying, defining, describing, narrating, or short answers to wh- questions (*This is a door.*).
* **Certainty**: definite agreement, firm disagreement, firm denials, firm rejection of an offer, or statements of absolute obligation (*That’s quite right.*, *You must eat your dinner.*).
* **Interrogative sentences (Yes/No)**: when an answer is actively demanded (*Have you seen this man before?*), or inside requests acting as orders (*Will you please be quiet.*).
* **Tag & Choice questions**: tag questions inviting agreement to a statement not in doubt (*This tastes nice, doesn’t it?*), and closed choice question lists (*Would you prefer tea or coffee?*).
* **Wh- questions & Imperatives**: a definite request for information (*Where is the toilet, please?*), direct orders, instructions (*Push to open the door.*), or strong forms of an offer.

### 2 High falling [ ' ]
Starts on a pitch high above the mid-point and drops low. Used for:
* **Declarative sentences**: exclamations indicating surprise, protest, enthusiasm, or emphasis (*That’s excellent!*, *You are hurting me!*, *Fancy that!*); or to indicate strong contrast with an element previously mentioned or assumed (*No, it was in Oxford that he studied.*).
* **Interrogative sentences**: insisting on an answer (*Did you post that letter?*), showing surprise/irritation (*Are you still not ready?*), rhetorical exclamations (*Isn’t she beautiful?*), or tags insisting on hearer's agreement (*I told you, didn’t I?*).
* **Imperative sentences**: insisting on an order where compliance is doubted (*Stop it, I say.*), urgent instructions during danger (*Stop. Don’t move.*), or insisting on an offer (*Do let me help you.*).

### 3 Low rising [ , ]
Starts on a clear, low level pitch and glides upward to mid-level. Used for:
* **Declarative sentences**: conveying indifference, resentment, guardedness, or suspicion (*It doesn’t matter.*, *You shouldn’t blame me.*); or to provide reassurance (*There’s no need to be worried.*).
* **Interrogative questions (Yes/No)**: politely asking for confirmation (*You’re French, aren’t you?*), making polite requests/offers (*Would you please open the window?*), or inside open choice lists (*tea or coffee or something stronger?*).
* **Wh- questions & Imperatives**: indicating polite interest rather than a strict interrogation demand (*Where are you spending your holidays?*); gentle commands to children or hospital patients (*Just drink this medicine nicely.*).

### 4 High rising [ \‘ ]
Starts between low and mid-level and glides high above mid-level. Used for:
* **Declarative sentences**: converting a basic statement into a question (*You were born in Scotland?*); querying or repeating what someone has said (*You say you’re thirsty?*).
* **Interrogative questions**: indicating a casual enquiry (*Care for a sandwich?*); repeating a question before answering it (*A sandwich? Would I care for a sandwich?*).
* **Wh- questions & Imperatives**: repeating a question before answering it (*Where do I live?*); asking for immediate repetition of information not heard or understood (*He lives where?*, *Where does he live?*); repeating an order, instruction, or offer while deciding how to comply (*Sit down? Why not?*).

### 5 Falling–rising [ v ]
Starts at a high-mid pitch, drops to a low creak, and glides upward to mid-level. Used in declarative, interrogative, and imperative environments to convey critical implications:
* **Warnings**: *That jug is hot!*, *Watch where you’re going.*
* **Corrections**: *Her dress isn’t blue, it’s green.*
* **Demurral & Limited agreement**: indicating mental reservations or limited agreement with major underlying disagreement (*I don’t know if I agree with that.*, *Yes, he is an active person.*).
* **Uncertainty & Hesitation**: *Yes, possibly. I can’t be certain.*, *Seven eights are fifty four?*
* **Softening bad news or conflict**: *You haven’t done very well, I’m afraid.*, *You’re wrong, you know.*
* **Anxious query & Discouragement**: *You do love me, don’t you?*, *You can go to the cinema if you like.*
* **Tentative advice**: *If I were you, I’d think about it.*
* **Unsaid mental reservations**: implying that something left unsaid contradicts or contrasts with the overt text (*Your opinion is interesting.* — implying *but I don’t agree*).
* **Scepticism / Pleading**: querying a statement to indicate doubt, or pleading for a turn (*Do try to be a little more careful.*).

### Head, Rhythm, and Phrasing
Where a tone group contains more than one prominent syllable, the first is the **head** (marked via an upright line [\`|]). It establishes a rhythmically stable pitch jump up to a high-mid level, sounding cheerful and friendly the higher the level. Non-prominent stressed syllables (marked with a mid-letter dot [\`·]) maintain rhythmic beats without altering the pitch pattern. 

Longer compound or complex sentences combine multiple tone groups into a **major tone group** (completion marked via [\`‖]), separated internally into constituent **minor tone groups** (marked via [\`|]). Common structural sequences include:
1. *Unemphatic, non-contrastive sentences*: Low rising minor group followed by a low falling final group (*When you see John | tell him to phone me.‖*).
2. *Contrastive sentences*: Falling-rising minor group followed by a high falling final group (*But when you see Harry | tell him I’ve left the country.‖*).
3. *Main statement and modifier*: Low falling group followed by a low rising modifier group (*I’m leaving for Germany | on Friday.‖*).
4. *Main statement and supplement*: Low falling group followed by a low falling supplement group.
5. *Apposition & Repetition*: Appositional phrases and repetitions using the word *too* repeat the exact nuclear tone of their antecedent nucleus (*John Smith, | a computer programmer | lives in Cambridge, | a university city.‖*, *His brother lives there, | too.‖*).
