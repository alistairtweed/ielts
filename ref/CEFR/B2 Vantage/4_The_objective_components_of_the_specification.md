# 4 The objective: components of the specification

Vantage, like its predecessors Threshold and Waystage, is conceived as a contribution to improved communication, particularly among Europeans of all backgrounds. A communicative approach aims to enable the learners to use a foreign language for their own purposes. What these purposes are depends on the personality, the circumstances, the needs and interests of the learners themselves. They are never fully predictable, but, starting from a particular target group, we can make an attempt to identify those things that all of them are at least very likely to need or wish to be able to do in the foreign language. In order to do this in any useful way we have to try to determine in what situations they are most likely to use the foreign language, what roles they will play in these situations, and what matters they are most likely to have to be able to deal with in the foreign language.

Determining all this is, in a way, a matter of guesswork. However, we can make better educated guesses if we make use of our collective experience, our knowledge of the world, and of whatever amount of consensus would appear to have been achieved. By and large, the assumptions made in the original Threshold Level would seem to have been widely upheld, so that the basis upon which the present specifications are built is a more solid one than for that earlier version. Yet, it should be constantly borne in mind that these assumptions are made with regard to what the members of the very large target group are supposed to have in common and that the undoubtedly considerable individual differences among these members are deliberately left unspecified. Vantage is a general objective only, and, moreover, one that is never to be regarded as fixed and closed but as something to be used flexibly and creatively.

Our starting-point remains the situations in which the learners are most likely to find themselves. Each situation will make its own demands on their communicative resources. At the same time, these demands have a lot in common, something which requires what we may regard as general communicative ability. An economical description, then, of what the learners need to be able to do, will specify the components of this general communicative ability plus, for each situation envisaged, the specific ability required to function adequately in it. The general ability, the ability required in most communication situations, will be the subject of by far the greater part of our specification. In fact, there will be only one component that is directly concerned with specific situation-related ability, and this will largely be a matter of concrete vocabulary items.

A communicative approach does not consider knowledge of the language as an end in itself. Its goal is the ability to use language, to do with language the kind of things one needs or wants to do with it. The starting-point of the specification of our objective, then, is a list of the kind of things people may do by means of language (such as describing, enquiring, denying, thanking, apologising, expressing feelings, etc.). We refer to these things as **'language functions'**. The first component of our specification (Chapter 5) is a list of those language functions that the members of our target group are likely to need to be able to fulfil.

Language functions are not fulfilled in a void. The concepts that we may refer to while fulfilling language functions will be indicated here as **'notions'**. Among the notions we distinguish 'general notions' and 'specific notions'. **General notions** are such as may be expressed in almost any situation (such as time, place, quantity, quality, or relations between entities) and are listed in Chapter 6. **Specific notions** are transaction-related and/or topic-related concepts typically expressed in particular situations only (such as *timetable* in travelling, or *potatoes* in eating). Specific notions are listed in Chapter 7 and exemplified in Appendix A.

At Vantage level, learners can be expected to have progressed beyond Threshold level in an enriched vocabulary for dealing with concrete details. While the common core of language learning is consolidated, learners will undoubtedly wish to discuss their special leisure, cultural, and professional interests in greater depth. We cannot specify the vocabulary needed for these diverse individual purposes as a general learning objective; we can only identify the categories within which learners will develop a vocabulary unique to themselves.

By specifying language functions, general notions and specific notions in relation to themes and tasks, we identify the basic elements of communication, which are usually combined within sentences. At Vantage level, greater control over greater linguistic resources enables the learner to rise above stereotypical schemata and to make more varied, flexible and effective use of principles of discourse structure and verbal exchange (treated in Chapter 8). It is expected that the spoken and written texts with which learners will wish to deal will be longer, richer and more various than was the case at Threshold level. Chapter 9 concerns authentic reading and listening text types, while Chapter 10 focuses on the place of authentic writing (primarily letters of all kinds and informal notes) in daily life.

Furthermore, the Vantage learner will be more familiar with community social conventions and able to act more flexibly with regard to formal and colloquial registers, politeness conventions, and cultural differences (Chapter 11). When language resources fall short, successful communication will depend on employing **compensation strategies** (Chapter 12). The individualised learning involved requires learners to progressively take charge of their own learning; the insights, skills, and attitudes that enable autonomous functioning are purposively pursued as core objectives in Chapter 13.

Finally, qualitative development is at least as important as quantitative development. While the Threshold criterion is the basic success of communication, the Vantage learner improves the quality of performance, looking to reduce the gap between the foreign language and their mother tongue. Chapter 14 discusses these skill parameters of accuracy, appropriacy, and fluency as criteria. Following a discussion in Chapter 15 of valuable 'by-products', appendices are devoted to suggested lexical exponents of specific notions (Appendix A), grammar (Appendix B), and pronunciation/intonation (Appendix C).
