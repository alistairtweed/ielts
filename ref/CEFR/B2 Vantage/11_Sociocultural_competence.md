# 11 Sociocultural competence

Sociocultural competence is that aspect of communicative ability which involves those specific features of a society and its culture which are manifest in the communicative behaviour of the members of this society. These features may be classified as 'universal experiences', 'social rituals' and 'social conventions'.

A particular problem in the case of the English language is that there is not one single sociocultural context in which it is used as a native language, but that there are several. The sociocultural contexts in which American English, Australian English, British English, South African English and many other 'Englishes' are used differ considerably in many of their distinctive features. In the present study we follow European educational tradition by requiring the learners primarily to be familiar with British English in its own sociocultural context.

## I Universal experiences
Learners are aware of the major differences between their own culture and that/those of the British Isles and sensitive to the diversity of other cultures in respect of:
1. **everyday life**: at what times people have their regular meals and in what ways the composition of meals, including beverages, typically differs from that in their own country; major national holidays; working hours; preferred leisure activities; normal patterns of domestic routine.
2. **living conditions**: living standards in relation to income, accommodation, education, social welfare, etc., including significant differences between social classes and major parts of a country, particularly Britain; ethnic composition of the population.
3. **interpersonal relations**: class structure of society and relations between the classes; relations within the family and between generations; formality/informality in work situations and in contacts with officials; interracial relations; major political groups.
4. **major values and attitudes**: social class; wealth and security; tradition; national identity and foreigners; politics and social affairs; religion.

## II Social conventions and rituals
1. **non-linguistic**:
   * *body language*: The learner is aware of the diversity of conventions in different countries with regard to hand shaking, touching, embracing, kissing, gesticulation, close physical proximity and protracted direct eye contact. He/She is familiar with the conventions generally adhered to in Britain.
   * *visiting rituals*: As visitors, the learners know within what limits they are expected to be punctual; whether they are expected to bring a present; what sort of clothes are appropriate; whether to expect refreshments or a meal; how long they are expected to stay and how to take leave.
   * *eating and drinking rituals*: The learners are familiar with the rituals generally current in Britain.
2. **linguistic**: The learner is aware of the sociocultural conventions governing the use of the language functions listed in Chapter 5, and can perform them appropriately.

## III Politeness conventions
The increased resources available to Vantage learners and their greater control over them make it possible for them to go beyond a relatively simple and direct way of using the foreign language. Politeness conventions in British English are particularly focused on negative politeness (avoiding embarrassment, conflict, or forcing the partner), and can be embodied in a number of maxims:

1. **Do not be dogmatic**: Remember that the partner may have a different opinion. It implies qualifying simple declarative sentences via the use of *I think, I believe, I expect*, the use of *you know, of course* to imply that the partner is not ignorant, and tag questions to invite agreement or confirmation. Correcting is presented gently by apologising for correcting, querying what has been said, presenting the correction as a different opinion, or requesting confirmation via a tag.
2. **Be reluctant to say what may distress or displease the partner**: This applies to breaking bad news, expressing disagreement, declining offers, or withholding permission. Strategies include expressing reluctance (*I don't want to complain but...*), seeking agreement, apologising/regret (*I'm afraid you haven't passed*), using euphemisms (*Your work isn't very good* instead of *bad*), and implying something unpleasant rather than stating it openly.
3. **Do not force the partner to act**: Allow him/her to appear to act voluntarily. Add *please* when calling for action, and avoid simple imperatives in favor of asking about willingness (*Will you...?*), ability (*Can you...?*), or wishes (*Would you like to...?*), using introducers (*I wonder if...*), or drawing attention to the situation (*It's cold in here* instead of *Close the window*).

Offers and invitations are handled dynamically. A 'strong' offer/invitation makes it easier to accept (*Let me help you*, *You must let me carry that case*). A 'weak' offer makes it possible to decline (*Would you like some help?*). A weak offer/invitation is not usually accepted without demur; commonly, a repeated offer is invited (*Won't that be too much trouble?*), which the partner can then either withdraw politely or reinforce (*No, really. I'd like to help*).
