# Appendix B: Grammatical summary

This summary presents a classified inventory of the grammatical categories, elements, and structures valid for Vantage level. Many structures found only in fixed formulaic expressions at Threshold level are generalized at Vantage level for independent use, ensuring greater structural accuracy, fluency, and flexibility across flowing speech and writing.

## A Word level

### A1 Nouns
* **1.1 Types of noun**:
  * **1.1.1 Proper nouns**: Capitalized names of days, months, seasons, continents, titles, persons, institutions, brand names, and book titles. Relevance depends on individual experience; learners must be able to recognize, confirm, store, and spell them out.
  * **1.1.2 Common uncountables**: Abstract entities (arts, sports, numbers, colours, compass points), mass materials (drinks, foodstuffs, means of transport), and verbal nouns / gerunds (*heating, parking*).
  * **1.1.3 Common countables**: Individual objects and living entities (*family members, animals, clothes, household articles, accidents*).
* **1.2 Number**:
  * **1.2.1 Regular written plurals**:
    * Adding *-es* after sibilants or final *-o*: *addresses, boxes, quizzes, brushes, matches, tomatoes*.
    * Consonant + *-y* shifts to *-ies*: *ladies*.
    * Substitution of final *-f(e)* to *-ves*: *wives, thieves*.
  * **1.2.2 Regular spoken plurals**:
    * Suffix /iz/ after sibilant roots: *glasses, sizes, dishes, garages, churches, judges*.
    * Suffix /s/ after voiceless stops/fricatives: *stops, adults, backs, laughs*.
    * Suffix /z/ in all other contexts: includes voicing changes like *baths, knives*.
  * **1.2.3 Irregular plurals**: *children, feet, men, teeth, women, pence*.
  * **1.2.4 Plural-only nouns**: *clothes, means, people, scissors, tights, trousers*.
  * **1.2.5 Singular-only nouns**: *information, luggage, furniture, news, sugar, unemployment*.
* **1.3 Genitive & Compounds**:
  * Singular nouns receive *'s* (*the man's head*); irregular plurals receive *'s* (*the children's room*); regular plurals ending in *-s* receive an apostrophe only (*my aunts' house*).
  * **Compounds**: Formed by two elements, main-stressed on the first element which modifies the head word. Can be single words (*newspaper*), hyphenated (*waiting-room*), or separate (*pocket money*). Valid primarily for human/animate entities; *of-adjuncts* are typical otherwise.

### A2 Pronouns
* **2.1 Types**: Demonstrative (*this, that, these, those*), Personal subject (*I, you, he, she, it, we, they*), Personal non-subject (*me, you, him, her, it, us, them*), Possessive complement/subject (*mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs*), Relative (*who, whom, which, that*), Interrogative (*who, whom, what, which, whose*), Reflexive/Emphatic (*myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves*), Propword (*one*), Indefinite (*somebody, someone, something, anybody, anyone, anything, nobody, no-one, nothing, everybody, everyone, everything, some, all, any, none, each, both, it, you*).
* **2.2 Gender usage**: Masculine (*he, him*), feminine (*she, her*), or neuter (*it*). Plural forms are non-sex-specific and are frequently used anaphorically after singular generic personal antecedents to ensure gender neutrality (*A good teacher will always try to help their students...*).

### A3 Determiners
* **3.1 Definite article**: Written *the*. Pronounced /ðiː/ before vowels or when stressed; unstressed /ðə/ before consonants; unstressed /ði/ before vowels. Used anaphorically, generically, or with uniques/situational contexts.
* **3.2 Indefinite article**: Written *a* before consonants, *an* before vowels. Used for unspecified items, frequency indicators (*twice a day*), or price-amounts (*35p a pound*).
* **3.3-3.10 Sub-classes**: Demonstrative, Possessive (*my, your*), Relative (*whose*), Interrogative (*what, which*), Indefinite quantitative (*some, any, no, every, much, many, more, most, several, few*), Distributive (*each*), Identifying (*another*), Pre-determiners (*all, such*), and Post-determiners (cardinal numerals, ordinal numbers, *other*).

### A4 Adjectives
* **4.1 Participial**: Active/present (*a working mother, boring*); passive/past (*a closed door, broken*).
* **4.2 Positioning**: Attributive-only (*daily, weekly, main*); Predicative-only (*alive, all right, ill, well, so-so*); or dual-use.
* **4.3 Grading**: Non-gradable (numerals, materials, categories like *married, open*); Gradable polar (*old/young, long/short*) or non-polar (*colours, tastes*).
* **4.4 Comparison**: Equality (*such, like, same as, as + adj + as*); Inequality (*different from, not so... as*); Comparatives (suffix *-er*, *-ier*, or *more + adj*; negative shift *less + adj*); Superlatives (suffix *-est*, *-iest*, or *most + adj*); Irregular combinations (*good/better/best, bad/worse/worst*).
* **4.5 Complementising adjectives**: Adjectives systematically triggering clause or phrase structures matching probability (*possible*), certainty (*sure*), evaluation (*wrong*), emotional state (*happy, glad*), moral obligation (*allowed*), or physical quality (*hard to lie on*).

### A5 Adverbs
* **5.1-5.2 Core types & Forms**: Simple forms or suffixed *adj + -ly*. Fulfill existential, temporal, locative, manner, degree, directional, sequence, frequency, or transition functions.
* **5.3-5.4 Categories & Comparison**: Indefinite (*anywhere, always, never*), Deictic/Anaphoric (*here, there, now, then*), Interrogative/Relative time-space-manner-reason indicators (*when, where, how, why*). Comparison follows regular monosyllabic suffixation (*faster, fastest*) or polysyllabic expansion (*more/most gladly*), plus irregular sets (*well/better/best, badly/worse/worst, far/further/furthest, little/less/least*).

### A6 Prepositions
* Classifies positional, spatial, temporal, manner, agentive, instrumental, benefactive, possessive, or inclusive structures (*above, at, behind, near, across, from, during, since, by, with, through, for, of, without*). Vantage learners manipulate these systematically inside clausal environments, alongside transparent phrasal structures.

### A7 Verbs
* **7.1 Forms**: Regular infinitive, present participle (*-ing*), past participle / past simple written suffix *-ed*, or spoken suffix variants (/id/ after /t, d/; /t/ after voiceless sibilants/stops; /d/ otherwise).
* **7.2 Irregular Paradigms**: Exhaustive operational awareness of primary irregular auxiliary and lexical base forms: *be, have, do, become, begin, break, bring, buy, choose, come, cost, cut, draw, drive, drink, eat, fall, feel, fight, find, fly, forbid, forget, forgive, freeze, get, give, go, hold, hurt, keep, know, leave, let, lie, lose, make, mean, meet, pay, put, ride, ring, say, see, sell, send, shine, shoot, show, shut, sing, sleep, speak, spend, stand, steal, strike, swim, take, teach, tell, think, throw, understand, undo, wear, win, write*.
* **7.3 Aspectual Tense Configurations**:
  * *Simple Present*: General fact, permanent status, habit, future schedule, or cognitive/stative presentation (*This food tastes good*, *I know you are tired*).
  * *Simple Past*: Actions completed in a fixed past timeframe, or indirect tracking of historical present references.
  * *Present Perfect / Past Perfect*: Past actions leading to a present threshold, continuous actions starting historically (*I have known Peter for ten years*), or indirect reported references.
  * *Progressive (Continuous) Aspects*: Present/past/perfect continuous combinations tracing actions actively in progress, temporariness, or immediate future motion tracks (*We are driving to Scotland next week*).
  * *Passive Voice*: Agentless passives when the active agent is unspecified or irrelevant (*Smoking is not allowed*, *My purse has been stolen*). Used syntactically to topicalize the active direct object (*The train was delayed by fog*) or active indirect object (*I was given another room*).
  * *Modal Auxiliaries*: Full syntactic operation across positive and negative finite systems:
    * `can/could`: ability, capacity, polite requests, tentative advice, sensory experience (*I can see some animals*).
    * `may/might`: possibility, permission, formal introductions, structural interruptions.
    * `must`: logical necessity (*He must be Belgian*), obligation, prohibition.
    * `shall/should`: future formatting, making offers, suggestions, duty/expectation.
    * `will/would`: future schedules, structural promises, requests, invitations, hypothetical conditions (*If you had asked me, I would have come*), structural preference (*I'd rather drink coffee than tea*).

### A8 Conjunctions
* Co-ordinating: Conjunctive (*and* for sequence or addition, *but* for contrast, *as well as, as... as*), Disjunctive (*or* for inclusive/exclusive tracking).
* Subordinating: Complementising (*that, if* inside indirect environments), temporal (*after, before, since, until, when, while*), spatial (*where, in which*), manner (*how*), reason/cause (*as, because, since*), effect/consequence (*so, so... that*), conditional (*if, unless*), and relative (*what, who, whom, which, whose*).

## B Phrase level

### B1 Noun phrase (NP)
* **1.1 Form variants**: Pronoun; proper noun; plural indefinite; unspecified mass substance/abstract noun; determiner + noun; pre-determiner + determiner + noun; full string layout: `(pre-det) + (det) + (post-det) + adjective(s) + noun`.
* **1.2 Adjective Ordering**: Fixed semantic structural flow: `Quality + Size + Shape + Colour + Material` (e.g., *all those nice little dark blue woollen dresses*).
* **1.3 Nominalised Verb Phrases**:
  * *To-Infinitive*: Used as subject (*To kill people is wrong*), following complementising adjectives (*It is likely to rain*), with qualifiers of degree (*cool enough to drink*, *too old to travel*), or following stative/volition verbs (*want to become a doctor*, *remember to close the window*).
  * *Wh + to + Infinitive*: Triggers structural cognitive objects (*Do you know where to go?*, *Teach me how to swim*).
  * *VP Gerund*: Used as subject (*Swimming in the sea can be dangerous*), following core evaluation verbs (*prefers driving*, *enjoy travelling by air*, *stopped raining*), or inside prepositional structures (*leave without paying the bill*).

### B2-B6 Other phrase types
* **Adjective Phrases**: Predicative elements with modifiers (*not good enough*), adjuncts (*bad for you*), or degree markers (*a very beautiful and most intelligent woman*).
* **Pronoun Phrases**: Quantified or relative targets (*the largest ones*, *some of the butter*, *something to drink*, *someone I met*).
* **Verb Phrases**: Formatted across intransitive, copular, transitive, causative, inchoative, resultative, or factitive verbs, including complex particle separation layouts (*The wind blew the tree down* vs. *The wind blew hard down the valley*). Handles core formulaic short answers (*Yes, I am*, *No, they don't*) and pro-VP phrasing (*do so*).
* **Preposition Phrases**: Complex multi-word structural units acting as structural prepositions: *in front of, in the centre of, at the back of, at the side of, at the end of, to the right of, in the neighbourhood of, next to, far away from, out of*.

## C Clause level
* **C1-C2 Clauses**: Main clauses (isolated or coordinated), and subordinate clause varieties. Noun clauses handle *that*, *if*, or *wh-* indirect questions. Adjectival relative clauses manage restrictive vs. non-restrictive features (explicitly noted in writing via commas, and in speech via clean tone-group divisions: *I do not buy books which are boring* vs. *I do not buy books, which are boring*). Adverbial clauses cover structural markers for place, time, reason, effect, condition, or comparison.
* **Overloading constraints**: Vantage learners accurately track embedded subordination but avoid heavy multiple "nesting" structural separations between a main clause subject and its predicate which would overload cognitive memory.
